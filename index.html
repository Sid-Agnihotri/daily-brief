Quantum computing, stop 1 of 12

A qubit is a vector, not a coin mid-flip

A classical bit holds 0 or 1. A qubit holds a little arrow of length one, and measuring it turns that arrow into a 0 or a 1 with odds set by which way it points.

Most introductions say a qubit is “0 and 1 at the same time” and move on. Today’s source, Andy Matuschak and Michael Nielsen’s Quantum computing for the very curious, skips the metaphor (the authors find it close to meaningless) and teaches the actual math object instead. The good news: the object is small.

Bits store a number, qubits store a vector

A bit’s state is one of two numbers. A qubit’s state is a two-dimensional vector: two numbers stacked together, a point in what the essay calls state space. Two special vectors play the roles of 0 and 1:

|0⟩ = [1, 0]|1⟩ = [0, 1]

These are the computational basis states. The bracket notation, called a ket, is just a label meaning “this is a quantum state.” One common trip-up: |0⟩ is not the zero vector. It has length one and points along the first axis.

Superposition is a weighted mix

Any other qubit state is a mix of the two: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The weights α and β are called amplitudes. The essay’s running example, 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩, is simply the vector [0.6, 0.8]. “Superposition” is physics vocabulary for what a programmer would call a linear combination.

There’s one rule, the normalization constraint: the squared amplitudes must add to 1, so |α|² + |β|² = 1. Check the example: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1. In general amplitudes can be complex numbers, which is why the full picture is a sphere rather than the circle drawn here. That comes later.

Measuring turns amplitudes into odds

You never see α and β directly. Measuring a qubit gives a plain 0 or 1: 0 with probability |α|², 1 with probability |β|². For 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩ that’s 36% and 64%. Measuring also changes the qubit: afterwards it is |0⟩ or |1⟩, and the original amplitudes are gone. To estimate them you need many identically prepared qubits.

Why qubits are so hard to build

The essay also explains a tension behind a lot of quantum hardware news. A qubit has to be isolated so the outside world doesn’t disturb its state, yet controllable enough for you to change it on purpose. The authors’ example: a neutrino would hold a quantum state almost perfectly because it barely interacts with anything, but for exactly that reason there’s no practical way to steer it. Much of quantum hardware design is a negotiation between those two needs.

Worth keeping

  • A qubit’s state is a unit-length vector in a 2-D space, written α|0⟩ + β|1⟩.
  • The amplitudes obey |α|² + |β|² = 1. “Superposition” just means a linear combination.
  • Measuring gives 0 or 1 with probabilities |α|² and |β|², and leaves the qubit in that basis state.
Quantum computing for the very curious Andy Matuschak & Michael Nielsen, Quantum Country, 2019

Software engineering, stop 1 of 14

Eight things happen before a web page appears

Typing a URL kicks off a lookup, two handshakes, a request, some server work, a response and a render. Almost every later topic, from caching to load balancers, is about making one of these steps faster or skipping it.

“What happens when you type google.com and press enter?” is a classic interview question, and the GitHub repo what-happens-when answers it collaboratively, all the way down to keyboard interrupts. Worth keeping

  • Before any HTTP is sent, the browser has to find the server (DNS) and open an encrypted connection (TCP + TLS).
  • The server routes the request to code that builds a response; status codes like 200 and 304 tell the browser what happened.
  • Rendering repeats the fetch cycle for every image, script and stylesheet, so most performance work is about skipping steps.

AI, stop 1 of 13

A language model is a next-word machine with a very good map of words

LLMs turn words into long lists of numbers, refine those lists through dozens of layers, and output odds for the next word. Generating text is that one step on repeat.

Most explanations stop at “it predicts the next word.” Timothy B. Lee and Sean Trott’s explainer goes a level deeper without equations, and it’s still one of the clearest tours of a model’s insides. It was written in 2023. Models are far bigger now, but the core machinery is the same.

Words become coordinates

A model doesn’t see C-A-T. It sees a word vector, a list of hundreds or thousands of numbers. The authors’ analogy is latitude and longitude: two numbers place a city on a map, and nearby cities have similar numbers. Word vectors do the same thing in a space with far more dimensions, so words with related meanings land near each other. Cat sits near dog, kitten and pet.

Because they’re numbers, you can do arithmetic on meaning. Google’s 2013 word2vec project found that taking biggest, subtracting big and adding small lands closest to smallest. The same trick maps countries to capitals. It also absorbs human biases from the text it learned from, which remains an open research problem.

Layers sharpen each word’s meaning

A word like bank means different things in different sentences, so one fixed vector isn’t enough. An LLM passes the vectors through a stack of layers (GPT-3 had 96, with each vector 12,288 numbers long), and each layer edits them to add context: this his refers to John, this bank is the money kind. Each layer does two things:

  • Attention. Each word looks around the prompt for other words that matter to it and pulls in their information. The authors describe it as matchmaking: every word advertises what it’s looking for and what it offers, and good matches share information.
  • Feed-forward. Each word is then processed on its own by a large network that behaves like stored knowledge, patterns and facts absorbed in training. Research the article covers found one such layer in GPT-2 converting “Poland” into “Warsaw.”

A neat division of labour falls out of this: attention retrieves from your prompt, and the feed-forward layers retrieve from what the model learned.

Training: guess the next word, adjust, repeat

No one labels the training data. The model reads ordinary text, guesses each next word, and an algorithm called backpropagation nudges its billions of weights so the right word becomes a little more likely next time. GPT-3 did this over roughly 500 billion words. The surprise of the past few years is how much ability comes out of that single, simple objective once you scale it up.

Worth keeping

  • LLMs represent each word as a long vector, and words with similar meanings sit close together.
  • A stack of layers refines those vectors: attention pulls in context from the prompt, feed-forward layers add learned knowledge.
  • Training is next-word prediction over huge amounts of text, with backpropagation adjusting billions of weights.

Wildcard

Saturn grew a ten-sided storm, and no one knows why now

Saturn’s north pole has had a hexagon of clouds for over 40 years. Hubble has now confirmed a decagon around the south pole, and it seems to have formed only in the last few years.

On September 2, a team led by Agustín Sánchez-Lavega of the University of the Basque Country published a paper in Science Advances confirming a giant ten-sided wave in the ammonia clouds circling Saturn’s south pole. Astronomers had been looking for a southern twin to the famous northern hexagon in Hubble images since 1990.

Bigger, younger, restless

North hexagonSouth decagon
First seen1980s, by the Voyager probesFaint hints in 2023, clear in 2025
MotionPractically stationaryDrifts east at about 10 km/h
StabilityUnchanged for 40+ yearsStill evolving and strengthening

Both sit on jet streams, fast rings of wind around the poles. The decagon isn’t just a pattern in the cloud tops: Hubble’s different wavelengths see it at several altitudes, so it extends down through multiple layers of the atmosphere. It is also enormous.

Amateurs saw it first

  1. 1980sThe Voyager probes spot the hexagon over Saturn’s north pole.
  2. 2004 to 2017NASA’s Cassini orbits Saturn and sees no sign of a long-lived southern polygon.
  3. 2017 to 2023The south pole is tilted away from Earth. The decagon most likely forms out of view.
  4. 2023Faint hints of a ten-vertex shape appear in Hubble images, recognised later when the team looked back.
  5. 2024Amateur astronomers Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger, with Sánchez-Lavega, spot a wavy band in images shared to a university-run planetary image archive.
  6. August to September 2025Hubble shows a fully formed decagon.
  7. September 2, 2026The confirmation is published in Science Advances.

The open question

Why a polygon appeared now, after decades without one, is what the team can’t yet answer. For the northern hexagon, the leading explanation is that the jet stream meanders north and south as it circles the pole, and those meanders lock into a whole number of crests; spinning tanks of fluid in the lab have produced similar polygons. Whether the decagon settles down like the hexagon or breaks apart is what the next few years of observation should show, as Saturn’s southern summer keeps the pole in view.

Worth keeping

  • Saturn now has a ten-sided wave around its south pole, a counterpart to the long-lived north-pole hexagon.
  • It rides a jet stream, extends through several atmospheric layers, drifts east about 10 km/h, and each side is longer than Earth is wide.
  • It appears to have formed between 2017 and 2023, and why it formed now is unknown.
NASA’s Hubble announcement NASA, September 2, 2026
